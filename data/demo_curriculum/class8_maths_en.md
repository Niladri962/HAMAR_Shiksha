## Chapter 1: Rational Numbers

A rational number is a number that can be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q is not zero. Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (which is 5/1) and 0 (which is 0/1). A number such as 5/0 is not defined, so it is not a rational number.

The additive inverse of a rational number p/q is -p/q, because p/q + (-p/q) = 0. For example, the additive inverse of 3/5 is -3/5.

The multiplicative inverse (reciprocal) of a non-zero rational number p/q is q/p, because their product is 1.

## Chapter 1: Rational Numbers

To add rational numbers with different denominators, first write them with a common denominator. Example: 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

Rational numbers are closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication: the result is always a rational number. Division by zero is not allowed.

Between any two rational numbers there are infinitely many rational numbers. For example, between 1/4 and 1/2 we find 3/8.

## Chapter 2: Linear Equations in One Variable

A linear equation in one variable has one unknown, and the highest power of the unknown is 1. Example: 2x + 3 = 11.

To solve a linear equation we keep both sides balanced: whatever we do to one side we must do to the other. Subtract 3 from both sides of 2x + 3 = 11 to get 2x = 8. Divide both sides by 2 to get x = 4.

Always check the answer by putting it back: 2(4) + 3 = 8 + 3 = 11, which matches the right-hand side.

## Chapter 2: Linear Equations in One Variable

Word problems: Five notebooks cost Rs 150. Let the cost of one notebook be x. Then 5x = 150, so x = 150 / 5 = 30. One notebook costs Rs 30.

A common mistake is to move a number across the equals sign without changing the operation. In x + 7 = 15, subtracting 7 from both sides gives x = 8, not x = 22.

Transposing: a term that moves to the other side changes its sign. From 2x - 5 = 9 we get 2x = 9 + 5 = 14, so x = 7.
